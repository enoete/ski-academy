Six key terms

Learn these and half the test is already won

Paleo means “old” and lithic means “stone.” Paleolithic = Old Stone Age. Say it like that and you’ll never forget it.
🔍 How do we know anything about people who never wrote a single word?
They left clues: stone tools, the ashes of old campfires, animal bones with cut marks on them, and paintings on cave walls. Archaeologists read those clues the way a detective reads footprints. Every fact in this guide was worked out that way.

⏳ How long is 2.5 million years?

The green sliver is everything since 8000 B.C.: farms, pyramids, phones, you. The orange is the Paleolithic Age.

Paleolithic Age

About 99.6% of the human story happened in the Paleolithic Age.

Surviving in the Paleolithic Age

Life in a band of 20 to 30 people

Ama’s story · Part 1: Moving day

The berry bushes along the river were picked bare, and the deer had stopped coming down to drink. So one morning Ama’s mother rolled up the hide tent, her uncle shouldered the spears, and all twenty-five of them started walking: babies, grandparents, and Ama carrying the basket of flint. Nobody complained. This happened every few weeks. Home wasn’t a place. Home was the band.

Ama’s band had no map and no fields. So how did they know where to go next?

Historians call the earliest era of prehistory the Stone Age because it was the time when people used stone to make tools and weapons. The earliest part of the Stone Age was the Paleolithic Age.

🚶🏾

Always moving

They moved around in search of food. They were nomads.
👨‍👩‍👧‍👦

Small bands

They traveled in groups or bands of about 20 to 30 members.
🏹

Hunt and gather

Searching for food was their main activity, and it was often difficult. They had to learn which animals to hunt and which plants to eat.
🧭 So how did Ama’s band know where to go?
Memory. The elders knew where the nut trees dropped their nuts in autumn and where the reindeer crossed the river in spring. They knew which berries were safe and which would make you sick. That knowledge kept everyone alive, and it was passed down by talking, which is one reason spoken language mattered so much (you’ll see that in chamber 6).
A band of 20 to 30 is about the size of Adiaha's class. Imagine your whole class packing up and walking to a new place every time the berries ran out!

Finding food

Men and women performed different tasks

Ama’s story · Part 2: The long hunt

Ama’s father and uncles had been gone four days. They were following a bison herd across the plain, reading bent grass and hoofprints in the mud. Back at camp beside the stream, Ama and her mother filled baskets with hazelnuts and berries and kept the little ones away from the water.

On the fifth night the hunters came home tired, sore and empty-handed. And yet nobody in the band went to bed hungry.

Why not? Who was really feeding the band? (Flint has the answer at the bottom of this chamber.)

🏹 Men: far from camp

  • Hunted large animals far from camp
  • Used tracking methods to locate animals
  • Used clubs, or drove animals off cliffs
  • Over time developed traps and spears to improve their chances

🧺 Women: close to camp

  • Stayed near camp, which was often near a stream or other body of water
  • Looked after the children
  • Searched nearby woods and meadows for berries, nuts and grains

Everyone worked

Finding food was the key to the group’s survival, so everybody helped. Some scientists believe both men and women made decisions for the good of the group. They may even have hunted in monogamous pairs, which means a man and a woman hunted together to find food for their children, creating the first families.

Tools and culture

The invention that changed everything

Ama’s story · Part 3: Grandfather’s stone

Ama’s grandfather sat with a lump of grey flint between his knees. Crack. He struck it with a round river stone and a thin flake fell away. Ama picked it up and yelped: it had sliced her thumb as neatly as a knife. Her grandfather laughed. He had learned the trick from his grandfather, who had learned it from his.

That trick was handed down for more than two million years. How could breaking a rock be one of the greatest inventions ever?

Culture is the way of life for a group of people who share similar beliefs and customs. The methods and tools Paleolithic people used to hunt and gather were part of their culture.

🪨 Flint flaking

They learned that hitting flint with another hard stone made it flake into pieces. The pieces had sharp edges for cutting.

🪓 The hand axe

A large piece of flint tied to a wooden pole. Flint technology was a major breakthrough for early people.
🔪 Was a flint edge really as sharp as Ama found out?
Yes. A fresh flake is sharp enough to slice meat off a bone and cut through tough hide. Its cousin, a black volcanic glass called obsidian, breaks into edges so fine that some surgeons today still use obsidian blades for delicate operations. A sharp edge meant more meat, warmer clothes and better shelters. That’s why it was a breakthrough.

🌱 Why tools matter so much

This early technology influenced later farming technologies, and farming influenced the places where early people chose to settle. Better tools, more food, fewer moves, and eventually: villages.

Think of flint like the first app update. One small upgrade, and suddenly everything else became possible.

Fire!

Make it first, then learn what it gave them

Ama’s story · Part 4: The night the fire died

Rain blew into the mouth of the cave and the fire hissed out. In the sudden dark, Ama heard something large breathing just outside. Her little brother started to cry. Her mother didn’t panic. She felt in her pouch for two dry sticks and a heavy, shiny, yellowish stone.

What was that stone, and why was getting the fire back so urgent? Try it yourself.

🔥 Start a fire by friction

Early people rubbed two pieces of wood together. The wood became heated, charred, and produced fire. Keep tapping!

🪵🔥

Cold, dark, and something is growling out there. Get a fire going.

Two ways to make fire: friction (rub wood on wood) and sparks from a stone called iron pyrite struck against another rock. The sparks lit dried leaves or grass.
🪔 Making fire was hard work. Why not just keep one burning forever?
Many bands probably tried. When they moved camp, they could carry glowing embers wrapped in moss or tucked inside a shell or hollow horn, then blow them back into flame at the new camp. Starting a fire from nothing took skill and patience, so keeping one alive was the easier option.

Language and art

The first stories ever told

Ama’s story · Part 5: A hand on the wall

Deep in the cave, far past where daylight reached, Ama held a stone lamp burning animal fat. By its flicker her aunt was painting a bison (yes, that’s Flint). Then her aunt pressed Ama’s hand flat against the rock, filled her mouth with red paint and blew. When Ama pulled her hand away, its outline glowed on the stone.

Thousands of years later, in 1879, another young girl wandered into a cave in Spain, held up a light and looked at the ceiling.

Who was she, and what did she find?

Paleolithic people began to express themselves with spoken language for much the same reasons we do. Before that, they communicated through sounds and physical gestures. Language let them share information and emotions.

🎨 How they made paint

Early artists crushed yellow, black and red rocks and combined them with animal fat. They applied the paint with twigs and their fingertips, and later used brushes made from animal hair. They painted scenes of lions, oxen, panthers and other animals. Few humans appear in these paintings.

🤔 Why paint in the darkest part of a cave, where hardly anyone would see?
Nobody knows for sure, and that’s what makes it exciting. Historians have three main ideas: painting an animal might bring hunters good luck, the paintings might record the group’s history, or they were made simply to be enjoyed. Which one do you believe? There’s no wrong answer, and it’s a great thing to argue about at dinner.

The Ice Ages

Cold enough to build a highway between continents

Meanwhile, far to the east…

Thousands of miles from Ama’s cave, another band was following caribou across a cold, windy grassland. Their grandparents told stories of a sea that used to be here. Now there was only dry land stretching toward the sunrise. The caribou kept walking east, so the band did too. Nobody planted a flag. Nobody knew they had just walked onto a new continent.

Where did the sea go? Drag the slider and watch.

The ice ages were long periods of extreme cold that affected all of Earth. The most recent Ice Age began about 100,000 years ago. Thick sheets of ice moved across large parts of Europe, Asia and North America.

🧊 Drag the glacier slider

As the ice sheets, or glaciers, grew larger, the water level of the oceans was lowered. Watch what appears.

🌤️🧊

🌉 The land bridge

The low sea levels exposed a strip of dry land connecting Asia and North America. It was known as a land bridge, and it acted as a natural highway that allowed people to travel from Asia into North America. From there, Paleolithic peoples moved southward to settle in different regions.

🌊 How much water did the glaciers actually steal?
Enough to drop the oceans by about 120 metres (400 feet), taller than a 30-storey building. Some ice sheets were more than 3 kilometres (2 miles) thick. Today the land bridge, called Beringia, is underwater again, beneath the Bering Sea between Russia and Alaska.

🧥 How did people adapt?

Ice age conditions were a grave threat to human life. To survive the cold, humans had to adapt, or change, many areas of their lives:

🥩

Diet

They changed their diets, enriching meals with fat.
🛖

Shelter

They built sturdier shelters.
🧥

Clothing

They made warm clothing using animal furs.
🔥

Fire

They used fire to stay warm.

The last Ice Age lasted about 90,000 years, ending between 9000 and 8000 B.C. — right when the Paleolithic Age ends too.

Think of the ocean as a bathtub. The glaciers are a giant freezer stealing the water. Less water in the tub, and the floor of the tub shows: that floor was the land bridge.

Review questions

Answer out loud, then flip the stone

Ama’s story · Epilogue

Ama grew up, and so did her children, and their children’s children, for hundreds of generations. Slowly the ice melted and the world grew warmer. Someone noticed that seeds dropped near camp sprouted into new plants. With a warmer climate and better tools, some bands decided to stop walking. They planted, they stayed, and they built the first villages. The Paleolithic Age was over.
