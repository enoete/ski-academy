The name game & the rivers

Tap each block to decode the name.

“Mesopotamia” is a Greek word. It describes the place perfectly: the flat river valley between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers. Today, most of it is the country of Iraq.

Watch out, two different names! Your notes say the name means “Land of civilized kings.” That is the meaning of Sumer, the first civilization in Mesopotamia (the Sumerians called their land Ki-en-gir).
Mesopotamia = land between the rivers. Sumer = land of civilized kings. Learn both.

Why here? A river story

Imagine you are a farmer, 6,000 years ago… Every spring, snow melts in the mountains up north. The rivers swell and spill over their banks. When the water goes back down, it leaves behind a thick, dark mud called silt. Silt is like free fertilizer: plants love it. But summer is scorching and it barely rains. So you and your neighbours dig ditches and canals to carry river water to your barley fields. That is irrigation. More food means more people, more people means bigger villages, and bigger villages turn into the world's first cities.

City-states: tiny countries

Sumer was not one big country. It was a group of powerful, independent city-states. Each one was a city plus the farmland around it, with its own ruler, its own army and its own favourite god. Think of the islands of the Caribbean: close neighbours, similar culture, but each running itself. Tap a card to flip it.

Memory trick: the four big Sumerian city-states are Uruk, Ur, Eridu, Lagash: “Uncle Uruk Eats Lasagna.”
BCE counts backwards! BCE means “Before the Common Era.” Like a rocket countdown, the bigger the number, the longer ago. So 4500 BCE is older than 1900 BCE.

Story time: the king who wanted to live forever

The Epic of Gilgamesh is one of the oldest stories ever written down, and it's set in Uruk.

Gilgamesh was the king of Uruk, said to be two-thirds god and one-third human. He was strong but a bully, so the gods made a wild man named Enkidu to be his match. The two wrestled, and neither could win, so they became best friends instead. Together they travelled to the Cedar Forest and defeated the monster Humbaba.

When Enkidu died, Gilgamesh was heartbroken and terrified of dying too. He went on a long journey to find the secret of living forever. He finally found a magic plant at the bottom of the sea, but while he bathed, a snake slithered up and ate it.

Gilgamesh walked home with nothing. But as he came near, he saw the massive walls of Uruk that he had built, and he understood: people don't live forever, but the great things they build and the good they do can last. That's why the epic credits Gilgamesh with building Uruk's walls.

Assyria above, Babylon below

After Sumer, two big powers ruled Mesopotamia.

Assyrians ⬆

  • Lived in northern Mesopotamia
  • Cities: Assur and Nineveh (on the Tigris)
  • Built a vast and powerful military empire: iron weapons, chariots, siege towers
  • Bonus: King Ashurbanipal's library at Nineveh held thousands of clay tablets, including the Epic of Gilgamesh

Babylonians ⬇

  • Rose to power in southern Mesopotamia
  • City: Babylon (on the Euphrates)
  • Famous for rulers like Hammurabi and early law codes
  • Bonus: Babylon later had the blue-tiled Ishtar Gate and the legendary Hanging Gardens
Memory trick: Assyria = Above (north). Babylon = Below (south). Bonus: Assur starts with A, just like Assyria, and Babylon is the Babylonians' home.

Together, the people of Mesopotamia laid the groundwork for modern society with several groundbreaking advancements. That's the next section.

Inventions we still use

Pick an invention.

What
Oldest known writing system in human history
When
Around 3200 BCE
Who & where
Sumerian scribes in the city-state of Uruk
Why
A bookkeeping tool to track grain, cattle and rations

Cuneiform means “wedge-shaped” (Latin cuneus = wedge). Scribes pressed a sharpened reed, called a stylus, into soft clay tablets. Then the tablets dried hard in the sun.

A day in the tablet house. Meet Ludi, a young scribe-in-training in Uruk. Every morning he goes to the edubba, the “tablet house” (school). The teacher, called the “school father,” writes a line, and Ludi copies it again and again. Today, the temple sends him to count the storehouse: 10 sheep, 30 jars of barley, a ration of bread for each worker. Ludi presses a little picture of a sheep, then marks for the number. His tablet is a receipt! That's the big secret: writing wasn't invented for stories. It was invented to keep track of stuff.

Fix in the notes: one line says the Sumerians created “the world's first writing system based on 60.” Two separate things got mixed together. The writing is cuneiform. The based on 60 part is their number system. See the next tab.

The Sumerians counted in groups of 60 instead of 10. That's why we still have 60 seconds in a minute, 60 minutes in an hour and 360 degrees (6 × 60) in a circle.

Why 60? Because 60 is the ultimate “share fairly” number. Imagine 60 dates to share with friends. Tap a number of friends:

Memory trick: look at any clock. Every tick is a 5,000-year-old Sumerian idea.
From pots to chariots. The wheel was first used by potters (around 3500 BCE). A potter spun a flat round wheel with clay on top to shape perfect bowls and jars. Then someone had a brilliant idea: turn that wheel on its side, put an axle through it, and attach it to a cart. The Mesopotamians adapted it for transportation, on carts and chariots.

The early wheels were solid wood planks cut into a circle, not spokes. Spoked wheels came later, making chariots light and fast for war.

Memory trick: “Pots before chariots.” Pottery first, transport second.

The Code of Hammurabi is one of the earliest and most complete written legal codes, set up by King Hammurabi of Babylon (around 1754 BCE) to ensure justice across his empire.

How many laws
About 282
Written on
A tall black stone pillar (a stele), taller than a grown-up
Where now
The Louvre museum, Paris
Famous idea
“An eye for an eye”
Why carve laws in stone? Before Hammurabi, a judge could make up a punishment depending on his mood. By carving the laws into stone and putting them in public, everybody could know the rules, and the rules couldn't quietly change. At the top of the stone, Hammurabi is carved standing before the sun god Shamash, the god of justice, as if to say, “These laws come with the gods' approval.”
