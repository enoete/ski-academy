The Agricultural Revolution

The moment humans stopped chasing their food and started growing it. Flip the switch to time-travel.

The Neolithic Age

Tap the blocks to decode the name.

The Neolithic Age is the latest (last) period of the Stone Age. It began about 8000 B.C. and lasted until around 4000 B.C., about 4,000 years.

Memory trick: “8 to 4.” The Neolithic Age ran from 8000 to 4000 B.C., like a long school day from 8 to 4.

The story of the big thaw

About 14,000 years ago… The world was coming out of the last Ice Age. Giant sheets of ice melted, and Earth's temperatures rose. The earliest people were nomads: they moved from place to place, hunting animals and gathering wild plants, always following the food. As the climate warmed, many nomads moved into areas with a mild climate and fertile land. Then something brand new happened. Instead of moving on, people stayed in one place to grow grains and vegetables. Slowly, farming replaced hunting and gathering as the main source of food. At the same time, people began to domesticate (tame) animals, which carried goods and gave meat, milk and wool.

Where the Neolithic fits: the age ladder

Paleolithic“Old Stone Age.” Nomads, hunting and gathering, simple stone tools. (Paleo = old, like paleontologists who dig up old bones.)
Neolithic“New Stone Age,” 8000–4000 B.C. Farming, villages, tamed animals, polished stone tools.
Bronze Age3000–1200 B.C. People mix copper and tin to make bronze. Civilizations appear.
Iron AgeBonus: after about 1200 B.C., iron tools take over.

Why it's called a revolution

Not a war. A change so big it reshaped everyone's life.

A revolution is any change that has an enormous effect on people's way of life. Historians call the settled farming of the Neolithic Age the Agricultural Revolution. People turned from hunting and gathering in nomadic communities to farming and herding in established communities.

The REAL change
Hunting & gathering → systematic agriculture
Systematic agriculture
Organized growing of food on a regular schedule
The clever trick
Farmers saved some grains to plant instead of eating them all
Big claim
Some historians call it the most important event in human history
Think of it like a phone. Before smartphones, you needed a camera, a map, a music player and a phone. After, everything changed at once: how we talk, travel, shop and learn. Farming was humanity's first “everything changes” invention. Once people could grow food, they could build houses, villages, then cities, governments and writing. That's why farming is called the foundation of civilization.
Memory trick: hunter-gatherers ate the wild seeds; farmers saved the seeds. Eat it all = move on. Save some = stay put.

Life in a Neolithic village

Permanent homes, close to the fields and close to water.

People built permanent homes in villages. They placed villages near fields, so planting and harvesting was easier, and near water, especially rivers, for drinking, watering crops and animals, and fishing. Neolithic farming villages grew in Europe, India, Egypt, China and Mexico. The biggest and earliest have been found in Southwest Asia.

The benefits of settling down

Sedentary life: settling in one place permanently instead of moving around.

Settling down set off a chain reaction. Tap to knock over the next domino.

All six benefits from class

1
Greater security from weather and wild animals
2
Steady food supplies → healthier, growing population
3
Increased labor force
4
Food surplus → trading networks
5
Job specialization: weapons, jewelry, pottery, baskets, cloth
6
Roles changed: men became family and community leaders; women took up domestic roles
Memory trick: “Safe Food Feeds Surplus Skills & Roles.” Security, Food supply, Force (labor), Surplus, Specialization, Roles.

Surplus means extra, more than you need. When one farmer can grow enough food for many people, the others are free to become potters, weavers or toolmakers. That's specialization: training for a particular job.

From stone to copper to bronze

The end of the Neolithic Age.

In the late Neolithic Age, toolmakers created better farming tools as people needed them:

Hoe
For digging soil
Sickle
For cutting grain
Millstone
For grinding grain into flour
A lucky accident. Workers heated certain rocks in very hot fires and discovered melted copper oozing out. They experimented and made copper tools and weapons, which were easier to make and use than stone ones. Later, craftspeople in western Asia mixed copper with tin. The result was bronze, which was stronger than copper. Big breakthrough!

Civilizations emerge

By the Bronze Age, communities grew into civilizations: more complex ways of life.

Four great river valley civilizations appeared around 3000 B.C.: Mesopotamia, Egypt, India and China. (You already know Mesopotamia!) Every civilization shares four features:

Cities & governmentTo protect people and their food supplies. The first governments were monarchies: a king or queen who inherits the throne from their parents.
ReligionTo help explain life and the forces of nature.
Social structurePeople grouped by the work they did and how much wealth or power they had.
Writing & artTo pass on information and keep records.
Memory trick: “Can Royals Sing Well?” Cities & government, Religion, Social structure, Writing & art. And the rivers: “My Elephant Is Cute” = Mesopotamia, Egypt, India, China.
