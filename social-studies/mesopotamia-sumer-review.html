Lesson 1

The land between two rivers

The big idea: civilizations grow where the geography helps farming, trade, or both.

Mesopotamia is a Greek name that means “land between the rivers.” It's also called the Fertile Crescent. The two rivers are the Tigris and the Euphrates. They begin in the mountains of present-day Turkey and Kurdistan, then flow southeast through present-day Iraq into the Persian Gulf.

The land between them was mostly flat, with small, scrubby plants. The rivers gave people three gifts:

Gift 1
💧 Water for drinking and for crops
Gift 2
🟫 Floods left fresh soil, perfect for farming
Gift 3
⛵ Easy travel and trade by boat

Why boats beat walking

In ancient times it was easier to travel by boat than over land: boats can carry heavy loads, the river current pushes boats going downriver, and there were few roads.

Notice the green strip: that's the fertile soil the rivers left behind. The orange dots are Sumer's cities.

Lock it in. Read the map left to right in ABC order: Euphrates is west (left), Tigris is east (right). Both rivers run “downhill to the Gulf”: from the mountains in the north-west, southeast into the Persian Gulf.
Natural borders, “Mountains, Deserts, Gulf”: the Zagros Mountains to the east, the Syrian Desert and Arabian Desert to the west, and the Persian Gulf to the south.
Wait, borders but easy to invade? Yes, both are true. The mountains and deserts are far out on the edges. The land where people actually lived was flat and open, with few mountains or barriers to stop raiders.

Lesson 1

No wood, no stone

Mesopotamia had amazing soil, and almost nothing else.

Mesopotamia had no forests for wood. It also lacked stone and minerals, such as metals. So how did people build anything?

Problem
No wood or stone to build with
Solution
🧱 Mud for bricks and plaster
Catch
Mud buildings crumbled easily and needed repairs often
Problem
Few mountains or natural barriers: easy to invade, and raiders came to steal or conquer
Solution
🛡️ Mud walls around their villages
Problem
Still needed stone, wood and metal
Solution
🌾 Grow a surplus of grain and trade it
Surplus
More than you need for yourself
Who's in charge here? Digging canals, building walls and trading had to be done again and again, at just the right time. So community leaders began organizing groups of people to do the work. That's the first step from village to city, and from city to government.
Lock it in, “Mud, Mud, Grain”: mud for houses, mud for walls, extra grain to trade for everything else.
Lesson 2

Sumer and its city-states

The world's first civilization, in southern Mesopotamia, around 3000 B.C.

Sumer was the first civilization, in southern Mesopotamia, around 3000 B.C. Its people, the Sumerians, built the first cities in Southwest Asia, including Ur, Uruk and Eridu. These cities controlled the lower part of the Tigris and Euphrates valleys.

Why every city went solo. Picture each Sumerian city as an island, but surrounded by mud-flats and scorching desert instead of sea. Travelling and talking with neighbours over land was hard, so each city was cut off. Each one grew its own crops, made its own goods and took control of the land around it. That made each one a city-state: a city that governs itself and its surrounding territory. Kind of like how each Caribbean island runs itself.

Lesson 2

Gods, priests and kings

Climb the ziggurat one level at a time.

Sumerians believed in many gods: polytheism (poly = many, theism = belief in god). The gods helped explain forces of nature and human activities and talents. Each city-state had its own patron god and honored it with a huge temple called a ziggurat.

Ready to climb?

Press the button to go up one level.

Who's in charge? Three steps

1. Priests ruled the city-states→ 2. War heroes became kings→ 3. Rule became hereditary

Sumerian kings claimed they got their power to rule from the city's god. Over time, their rule became hereditary: when a king died, his son took over. A kingdom like that is a monarchy.

Lock it in: zig-ZAG your way up and you rise high. Ziggurat means “to rise high” in the Akkadian language, and only special priests may reach the top, the home of the gods.

Different from your notebook! Your earlier notes put farmers at the bottom with enslaved people. Lesson 2 puts farmers in the middle class, along with merchants, fishers and artisans. For this test, go with Lesson 2.
The Standard of Ur. A standard is a flag or symbol carried on a pole. The Standard of Ur is a decorated panel, only about 8.5 by 19.5 inches, made with shell and blue lapis lazuli. Its rows of tiny people show Sumerian society: kings and soldiers at the top, farmers bringing animals and goods below. It's like an ancient comic strip of who's who.

Roles of men and women

Men
  • Head of the home; provided for the family
  • Boys went to school and became apprentices, training for specific jobs
  • Cared for parents if they needed help
Women
  • Ran the home
  • Taught daughters to run a household
  • Took care of children and elderly parents
Lock it in, “Kings Pray, Warriors Obey” for the upper class: Kings, Priests, Warriors, Officials. Middle class is “My Farm Feeds All”: Merchants, Farmers, Fishers, Artisans. It's the biggest group. Enslaved people (war captives, criminals, people who couldn't pay debts) were the lowest.

Sumer's top 10

Later civilizations copied and improved these ideas. Some are still on your wrist and in your phone.

Lock it in, seven verbs: Write (cuneiform), Roll (wheel), Sail (sailboat), Plow (wooden plough), Spin (potter's wheel), Melt (bronze), and Count (base 60, times tables, 60-minute hour and 360° circle, 12-month lunar calendar).

Key words: cuneiform, scribe, epic

Cuneiform
Sumerian writing: wedge-shaped marks in soft clay
Scribe
A person who copies or writes out documents; often a record keeper
Epic
A long poem about the deeds of a legendary or real hero, like Gilgamesh

Night-before cheat sheet

Everything on one screen. Read it out loud once.

Geography
  • Mesopotamia = land between the rivers = Fertile Crescent
  • Tigris (east) & Euphrates (west)
  • Start: mountains of Turkey & Kurdistan
  • Flow southeast through Iraq to the Persian Gulf
  • Borders: Zagros Mts, Syrian & Arabian Deserts, Persian Gulf
River words
  • Floodplain: flat land by the banks
  • Silt: fine soil left by rivers
  • Fertile: good for crops
  • Semiarid: under 10 in. of rain, hot summers
  • Drought: long time with little or no rain/snow
  • Erosion picks up soil; deposition drops it
Taming the water
  • Floods unpredictable: April to June
  • Irrigation: canals carry river water to fields
  • Workers cleaned silt out of canals
  • Dams held back extra flood water
Resources
  • No forests, stone or metals
  • Mud bricks & plaster (crumbled easily)
  • Few natural barriers: mud walls
  • Surplus grain traded for stone, wood, metal
City-states
  • Sumer: 1st civilization, ~3000 B.C., southern Mesopotamia
  • Cities: Ur, Uruk, Eridu (also Kish, Nippur, Lagash)
  • Cut off by mud-flats & desert → independent
  • 5,000–20,000 people, own government, big wall
  • Palace + ziggurat in the center
  • War over resources & borders; trade in peace
Religion & rulers
  • Polytheism: many gods; each city has a patron god
  • Ziggurat = “to rise high” (Akkadian)
  • Top = home of the gods; only special priests
  • Priests → war-hero kings → hereditary kings
  • Kings' power came “from the city's god”
Classes
  • Upper: kings, priests, warriors, officials
  • Middle (largest): merchants, farmers, fishers, artisans
  • Lowest: enslaved people
  • You stayed in the class you were born into
Trade & inventions
  • Most Sumerians were farmers
  • Gave wheat, barley, tools; got timber, minerals, metals
  • Carnelian (red) from the Indus Valley; lapis lazuli (blue) from Afghanistan
  • Write, Roll, Sail, Plow, Spin, Melt, Count
